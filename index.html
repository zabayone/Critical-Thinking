Speaker notes — Slide 4 (≈55 s)

To understand the ethical stakes, we must first situate the technology. On the research side, models such as Google’s MusicLM demonstrated high-fidelity text-to-music generation, establishing proof of concept. On the commercial side, Suno and Udio made this capacity accessible to anyone in 2023.

In the conceptual terms of our course, we are dealing with what Chiffi calls a radical innovation — highly discontinuous, revolutionary in nature, and unfolding under deep, non-quantifiable uncertainty. As long as it remained research, ethical questions could be deferred. Once millions of users had access, deferral was no longer defensible.

Speaker notes — Slide 5 (≈55 s)

Four primary capacities: text-to-music (a prompt becomes a track); stylistic control (genre, mood, instrumentation); synthetic voice; and melodic continuation from a fragment.

Outputs already span from ambient textures to polished pop sketches, deployed in advertising, stock-music libraries, and budget-constrained media.

The critical point: these capacities are not ethically neutral. Each affects different human actors in different ways. Synthetic voice, in particular, touches on artistic identity — a point we will return to.

Technical distinction

Learning from music vs reproducing it

Learning

Models may abstract broad musical regularities comparable to how a human absorbs stylistic conventions through listening.

Reproduction

Alternatively, models may recombine distinctive stylistic fingerprints closely enough to produce derivative outputs.

This distinction matters ethically and legally because it marks the boundary between innovation and infringement.

Speaker notes — Slide 6 (≈60 s)

A technical distinction with heavy ethical implications.

Learning is what a human does who listens to a great deal of jazz and absorbs its conventions: the model abstracts general regularities.

Reproduction is the recombination of distinctive stylistic fingerprints — recognisable sonic signatures — closely enough to yield outputs derivative of identifiable artists.

This boundary is precisely the line between innovation and infringement. It is also a paradigmatic instance of what the course calls a wicked problem (Rittel and Webber 1973): the problem cannot be cleanly formulated independently of its resolution, because the answer depends on opaque technical parameters internal to the model.

Why insist on the technical picture before moving to ethics? Because without it, we cannot judge responsibility, we cannot locate harms, and we cannot separate hype from real risk.

This connects directly to what the course identifies as the problem of many hands (Noorman, in van de Poel): in a chain comprising designers, engineers, platforms, prescribers, and users, moral responsibility becomes distributed and diluted. Grasping the technical architecture is the precondition for re-allocating responsibility in a principled way.

With this foundation established, we can now enter the ethical core: who is used, displaced, or misrepresented by these systems, and how?

Ethical issue

Consent and training data

Artist concerns

Artists have publicly objected to systems trained on their work without permission, calling for rights, opt-out mechanisms, and redress.

Artist Rights Alliance · 200+ signatories · April 2024

Moral problem

Training without consent transfers value from creators to platforms without compensation, acknowledgment, or choice.

Kant: reciprocity principle violated

Speaker notes — Slide 8 (≈60 s)

First substantive ethical issue: consent. In April 2024, the Artist Rights Alliance open letter — signed by over two hundred artists, including Billie Eilish, Stevie Wonder, and Nicki Minaj — publicly denounced the use of their works to train these models without permission.

Morally, the problem is dual. From a Kantian deontological standpoint, recall Kant’s reciprocity principle: “Act so as to treat humanity, whether in your own person or in that of any other, in every case as an end, never as a means only.” Training on artists’ work without consent treats them as mere means — instrumentalised inputs to an extractive pipeline.

From a consequentialist standpoint — in Bentham’s tradition, the moral balance sheet of utilities and disutilities — we observe a transfer of value from creators to platforms without compensation or acknowledgement. Both frameworks converge: this is not merely a legal infringement, it is a moral wrong.

Ethical issue

Labor displacement and creative value

Who is affected?

Composers, producers, session musicians, sound designers, and vocalists all face substitution pressure.

Where first?

Budget-sensitive sectors: advertising, stock libraries, podcasts, social media, and game audio.

Why it matters

The cultural labor that trained these models can be undercut economically by those very same models.

Speaker notes — Slide 9 (≈60 s)

Second ethical issue: labour displacement. Composers, producers, session musicians, sound designers, and vocalists all face substitution pressure.

Where first? In the most cost-sensitive sectors: advertising, stock-music libraries, podcasts, social media, and game audio. These are precisely the “budget-sensitive sectors” where the marginal utility of a human professional collapses fastest.

The structural paradox is striking: the cultural labour that trained these models is the very labour those models now economically displace.

Applied to the course’s framework of virtue ethics: the question becomes not “what rule must I follow?” nor “what outcome must I maximise?” but “what character traits, what cultural habits, are we cultivating or undermining?” A society that automates creation must ask whether it still rewards craft, patience, and long-form apprenticeship — the very excellences (Aristotelian aretai) that constitute musical practice.

Speaker notes — Slide 10 (≈60 s)

Third ethical issue: authorship. The question “who wrote this song?” has at least four candidates — the prompt user, the engineers who trained the model, the artists whose works form the training set, or, most unsettling, no human author at all.

This is a textbook case of what Merel Noorman, drawing on van de Poel, calls the problem of many hands: responsibility — and here authorship, its constitutive correlate — is distributed across the entire chain of design, development, and use. Assigning it to any single agent is conceptually inadequate.

Legally, the U.S. Copyright Office’s 2025 report on AI and Copyright stipulates that AI outputs qualify for protection only when sufficient human expressive control is present. But the threshold remains contested, evolving, and difficult to operationalise.

Moral frameworks

Applying moral reasoning

⚖️Consequentialism

Bentham · utility principle

📜Deontology

Ask whether using human work without consent violates duties to respect and acknowledge creators.

🌱Virtue ethics

Consider what character traits and cultural habits we reward, or undermine, in music production.

Aristotle · aretai (excellences)

Speaker notes — Slide 11 (≈75 s) — KEY SLIDE

We now apply the three normative frameworks of the course to AI-generated music.

Consequentialism, in the tradition of Jeremy Bentham and classical utilitarianism: actions are judged by the balance of pleasure and pain they produce — the “moral balance sheet” in which costs and benefits of each possible action are weighed. On the benefit side: democratised access to musical creation, efficiency gains across the production chain. On the cost side: the weakening of creative ecosystems, the erosion of livelihoods, the cultural impoverishment of homogenised output. The balance is far from self-evidently positive.

Deontology, in Immanuel Kant’s formulation: we examine duties rather than outcomes. The categorical imperative in its second formulation — the reciprocity principle — commands us to treat humanity always as an end and never merely as a means. Using artists’ work without consent treats them precisely as mere means: instrumentalised inputs. Crucially, no aggregation of positive consequences can wash out this violation.

Virtue ethics, in the Aristotelian tradition: the question is neither “what rule must I follow?” nor “what outcome must I maximise?” but “what kind of agent, what kind of community am I becoming?” Do we still reward mastery, patience, long apprenticeship — the excellences proper to musical practice? Or do we reward indifferent, mass-produced output?

No single framework yields a closed answer. But, as Chiffi emphasises, “ethics is not a manual with definite answers: it is about questions and arguments concerning the moral choices people can make.” The plurality of frameworks is not a weakness — it is the discipline.

Case

Real-world conflicts: lawsuits and industry response

Major record companies initiated lawsuits alleging large-scale infringement in 2024. These disputes show the issue is institutional, not merely theoretical or philosophical.

RIAA · Universal · Sony · Warner v. Suno & Udio · June 2024

Why it matters

Legal disputes force platforms and labels to confront claims of unauthorized use with concrete accountability.

Next question

If listeners cannot tell AI from human, does that create an independent ethical problem beyond copyright?

Speaker notes — Slide 12 (≈55 s)

These ethical questions are not merely theoretical. In June 2024, the RIAA together with the three major labels — Universal, Sony, and Warner — filed suit against Suno and Udio, alleging large-scale infringement through unauthorised use of their catalogues for training.

Why does this matter? Because these lawsuits force platforms into concrete institutional accountability: the debate migrates from the philosophical seminar room into the courts — precisely the kind of forum, as Chiffi argues, where the political and democratic moralisation of technology can take place.

And these proceedings open the question my colleague will take up next: if listeners cannot perceptually distinguish AI from human music, does this create an independent ethical problem — one of deception and trust — that goes beyond copyright? A question that leads us directly to the Turing test applied to musical expression.

Conclusion

A practical framework for ethical AI music

The key issue is not whether AI should exist in music, but under which rules it can be used fairly.

✓ Consent

Artists should have control over whether their music is used in AI training datasets.

🏷 Disclosure

Listeners should be informed when music is AI-generated or when an artist identity is simulated.

🔍 Provenance

Watermarking and metadata can improve traceability and platform accountability.

💰 Compensation

If AI systems derive value from human music, creators should not be excluded from that value.

Ethical AI music is not just about innovation. It depends on transparency, permission, and fair distribution of value.
